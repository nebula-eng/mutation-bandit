# Evaluation

## 1. Setup

| item | value |
|---|---|
| host | Windows 11, Python 3.13.14 |
| per-run budget | 20,000 executions |
| step budget | 1,200 parser nodes |
| seeds | 1,2,3,4,5,6 |
| targets | TLV, JSON-lite, URL |
| policies | uniform, AFL fixed mix, AIMD repair control, contextual bandit |
| repetitions | 24 campaigns per policy (3 targets x 6 seeds) |

All numbers below are read from `results/results.json`; the file is the single source
of truth and is regenerated by `make eval`. No number in this document was typed by
hand from a different run.

## 2. Metric definitions

- TTFF: execution index at which the first seeded defect is reached. Runs that
  find nothing are censored at 20,000 and reported separately as detection rate.
- Deep-defect reach: fraction of seeds that reach at least one defect in the
  target's designated deep set (TLV: crc bypass, length wrap, nested depth; JSON:
  depth overflow, number overflow; URL: port truncation, host length).
- Edges: cumulative virgin-bit count at the end of the campaign, a proxy for
  exploration, not for defect discovery.

## 3. Time to first defect

Median over six seeds, in executions. Lower is better.

| target | uniform | AFL fixed | AIMD repair | contextual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TLV | 1242.5 | 819.5 | 311.0 | 534.5 |
| JSON-lite | 1026.0 | 1417.0 | 1196.5 | 3546.0 |
| URL | 190.0 | 125.0 | 149.0 | 92.0 |
| pooled (geo. mean) | 623.4 | 525.5 | 381.3 | 558.7 |
| detection rate | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

The AIMD controller is 1.38x faster than the AFL-style mix and 1.63x faster than
uniform when pooled. The effect is not uniform across targets: it is largest on TLV
(2.6x vs uniform), inverted on JSON (1.18x faster than AFL, 1.17x slower than
uniform), and slightly negative on URL against AFL (149.0 vs 125.0) while still
better than uniform.

## 4. Deep-defect reachability

Fraction of six seeds reaching a designated deep defect.

| target | uniform | AFL fixed | AIMD repair | contextual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TLV | 0.67 | 0.50 | 0.33 | 0.83 |
| JSON-lite | 0.17 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.50 |
| URL | 0.83 | 1.00 | 0.83 | 0.83 |
| mean | 0.556 | 0.500 | 0.389 | 0.722 |

This is the central trade-off. The policy that minimises first-defect latency
(AIMD repair) has the *worst* deep-defect reachability. The policy that maximises
depth (contextual bandit) is the slowest to the first defect on JSON by a factor of
3.5 against uniform. Optimising one objective measurably degrades the other.

Mechanism: the repair controller raises `p_repair` whenever inputs die early, which
concentrates the budget on short, valid inputs: good for a shallow defect, bad for
accumulating the payload growth that length-overflow defects require. The contextual
bandit instead keeps destructive operators in play once progress is high, which
preserves the long inputs needed for depth.

## 5. Coverage

Mean cumulative edges at 20,000 executions:

| target | uniform | AFL fixed | AIMD repair | contextual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TLV | 18.5 | 18.0 | 18.0 | 18.0 |
| JSON-lite | 33.5 | 33.0 | 33.0 | 32.0 |
| URL | 19.0 | 16.0 | 17.0 | 16.0 |

Coverage is nearly saturated by every policy. It is reported for completeness and
deliberately not used as the headline metric: at this target size, edge count cannot
distinguish policies, which is itself part of the finding.

## 6. Rejected: context-free operator bandits

The original hypothesis was that a UCB1 bandit over operators would beat a fixed mix.
Same grid, 20,000 executions, six seeds. `uniform` and the sliding window do not
consult measured cost, so they reproduce exactly; UCB1 divides its success rate by the
measured cost of each arm, so the command was run twice and both repeats appear:

| target | uniform | UCB1 run 1 | UCB1 run 2 | sliding-window UCB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TLV | 1242.5 | 1184.5 | 1156.5 | 702.5 |
| JSON-lite | 1026.0 | 1585.5 | 2227.0 | 2090.5 |
| URL | 190.0 | 128.5 | 77.0 | 138.0 |
| pooled | 623.4 | 622.6 | 583.2 | 587.4 |

Deep-defect reach, same runs:

| target | uniform | UCB1 run 1 | UCB1 run 2 | sliding-window UCB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| TLV | 0.667 | 0.333 | 0.667 | 0.500 |
| JSON-lite | 0.167 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.167 |
| URL | 0.833 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.833 |

Pooled, every policy lands within 7% of uniform, so the honest summary is that
operator-level scheduling neither helps nor hurts on aggregate. Two things do differ.

The composition moves. UCB1 is 1.5x to 2.2x slower than uniform on JSON-lite and
loses deep reach there entirely (0.167 to 0.000), while reaching one to three URL
defects faster. A pooled geometric mean reports a wash and hides that; the per-target
rows are the result.

The measurement is unstable. The same command run twice moves JSON-lite from 1585.5
to 2227.0 executions, a 40% swing, because the efficiency term divides by a cost that
is measured rather than modelled. An operator policy that responds to timing jitter is
not a policy a reader can check, which is reason enough to leave it out of the
headline table.

The diagnosis for the first point stands: reward is sparse, most pulls add no edge, so
the exploration bonus dominates the estimated means and the policy concentrates on a
few arms. In fuzzing input diversity is cheap to produce, and JSON-lite is the target
that needs a long, well-formed input most. The sliding window recovers the TLV
regression and stays stable, but it does not beat uniform on JSON-lite either.

Both repeats are in `results/ablation_ucb.json`; `scripts/ucb_ablation.py` regenerates
them.

## 7. Corpus admission ablation

The comparison is run at three equal budgets (5,000 / 10,000 / 20,000 executions),
with all four policies and six seeds, so every point is the mean of 24 campaigns.
"Coverage-only" admits an input on a new edge; "behavioural" also admits it on a new
(parse-progress, log2 size) cell. Deep reach is the fraction of campaigns reaching a
designated deep defect; detection is the fraction reaching any defect.

| admission | budget | JSON deep | JSON det | TLV deep | TLV det | URL deep | URL det |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| coverage-only | 5,000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.583 | 0.000 | 0.708 |
| coverage-only | 10,000 | 0.000 | 0.042 | 0.000 | 0.792 | 0.000 | 0.750 |
| coverage-only | 20,000 | 0.000 | 0.167 | 0.000 | 0.917 | 0.000 | 0.750 |
| behavioural | 5,000 | 0.042 | 0.875 | 0.125 | 1.000 | 0.583 | 1.000 |
| behavioural | 10,000 | 0.042 | 1.000 | 0.333 | 1.000 | 0.792 | 1.000 |
| behavioural | 20,000 | 0.167 | 1.000 | 0.583 | 1.000 | 0.875 | 1.000 |

The curve is the point. Coverage-only admission stays at 0.000 deep reach on all
three targets at every budget: quadrupling the execution budget does not help,
because the corpus never retains a longer input. Detection creeps up (JSON 0.000 to
0.167) as random mutations eventually stumble into a shallow defect, but the growth
path stays closed. Behavioural admission is monotonic on every target, and detection
reaches 1.000 by 10,000 executions.

This is a larger effect than any scheduling choice measured here, and it is the
practical takeaway: what you keep in the corpus bounds what you can ever reach.
Raw numbers: `results/admission_sweep.json`; figure: `results/fig_admission.png`.

## 8. Cost

Median microseconds per execution, including mutation, scheduling and parsing. These
are wall-clock measurements, so they live in `results/timing.json` rather than
`results.json`:

| policy | TLV | JSON | URL |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| uniform | 24.7 | 16.5 | 34.6 |
| AFL fixed | 19.5 | 16.4 | 16.7 |
| AIMD repair | 24.9 | 19.3 | 21.8 |
| contextual | 35.8 | 44.6 | 20.7 |

The contextual policy is 1.4-2.7x more expensive per execution because it produces
longer inputs, not because the policy check is slow (a `choose` call is O(arms) with
seven arms). Cost is reported per execution rather than per wall-clock second so that
the comparison is machine-independent.

## 9. Threats to validity

1. Three targets, eleven defects. Small sample; per-target medians have wide
   uncertainty (six seeds). Pooled geometric means are reported to avoid over-reading
   a single target, but pooling hides the JSON/URL disagreement.
2. Python parsers. Per-execution cost and crash semantics differ from native
   code; the scheduling effect may not transfer to C targets with persistent forks.
3. Injected defects. Ground truth is exact, but the defect set is designed, not
   sampled from a real vulnerability distribution.
4. Step budget as timeout proxy. Non-terminating inputs are cut at 1,200 nodes.
   A real fuzzer would use a wall-clock timeout; the deterministic substitute changes
   which hangs are observable.
5. Single machine, single run. Seeds are fixed but the campaign is not repeated
   across machines. Timing numbers will differ elsewhere; defect counts will not,
   because execution is deterministic.
6. Reward signal. Only binary new-edge is used. A reward based on parse progress
   or rare-edge counts might change the bandit conclusions; this is untested and
   stated as such.
